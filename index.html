About

Comfort with people is built in the first weeks, long before you meet them.

I'm William, and I came to rabbits through chinchillas. A chinchilla doesn't want to be held. Keeping them taught me what I was after: an animal that comes to you.

These litters are Holland Lop crosses. Purebreds go on sale late October or early November, once I've seen which ones I'm keeping as they grow. A farmer sold me a Satin Angora, and a Holland Lop followed. The purebred lines are starting: the first purebred Holland Lops were born 18 September 2026, the first Netherland Dwarfs were born 6 October 2026, and another Angora comes after, kept separate. Every purebred has its full pedigree.

Some rabbits never go up for sale. Each bloodline is bred to get better, and the ones that carry it forward stay. Every litter goes on the litters page once I have its first photo, back to 28 June 2026.

A few rabbits at a time, on purpose. Selective breeding, then time on each animal, handled every day by me and by other people.

Rabbits are prey animals, and hands have to be learned. Babies handled briefly at feeding time in their first week come up to people at weaning, and still do as adults (opens in a new tab). Babies nobody handled avoid the hand and grow up afraid of people.

Those weeks come long before you could meet them, so that job is mine. I keep it going every day after. Every baby here is handled by at least ten different people before it goes home, and goes outside for new sounds and smells. A rabbit calm only with its breeder isn't socialized.

The routine is what you're paying for. It makes a rabbit that comes to people instead of running from them. It won't make one love being lifted: most never do (opens in a new tab), and each one is still its own animal.

Where the money goes. I buy my breeding rabbits from breeders I meet at shows across the country, and I fly home with them. A pedigreed rabbit from a winning line costs thousands. I pay it so that its babies can go to homes here for a fraction of that, and every sale on this site goes into the next trip and the next line.

Every rabbit here gets the same raising, whatever its price. The aim is a rabbit good enough for the show table that is just as happy on your couch. One day this is a ranch, and other animals get the same care. It starts with rabbits, done properly.

What happens before you meet them

The whole routine, week by week. Every listing shows which week its rabbit is in. Ask me about any part of it.

Week 1–2

First handling

Short, gentle sessions within days of birth, nest kept warm and mostly undisturbed.

Week 3–5

Carrying becomes normal

Lifting with a hand under the back and bottom every time, and long stretches at floor level, which is where a rabbit is most at ease. Leaving the ground is the fear most pet rabbits never lose.

Week 6–8

New people, new noise

Visitors, children, the noise of a house, time outside for new sounds and smells, and the carrier with the door open so it stops being a trap. At least ten different people handle each one before it goes home. Ordinary life at their own pace, with somewhere to retreat to whenever they want it.

Going home

When they're ready

Eight weeks is what I aim for. A rabbit goes home once it's off milk and eating hay and pellets on its own, and has been eating and drinking well for at least a week. I weigh it, and it has to be holding or gaining, with nothing wrong with it. One that isn't there yet waits, whatever its age.

I have cats or dogs. Can a rabbit work?

Often, yes, with slow introductions. Tell me what animals you have when you get in touch. If a rabbit isn't right for your home, I'll say so.

They've met noise before. While they're still working out what is safe, the babies here hear the noise of a house, go outside for new sounds and smells, and get handled by at least ten different people. Your home is one more new place, not the first.

Cats usually settle in. The House Rabbit Society says indoor cats typically will peacefully coexist (opens in a new tab) with rabbits after proper introductions. Start with the rabbit in a pen the cat can't reach into, and keep the cat's claws trimmed.

With a dog, it depends on the dog. A calm dog that doesn't chase small animals can learn, on a lead and lying down, in short visits. Terriers and hunting breeds are the hardest. Supervision is required until 100% trust has been achieved (opens in a new tab).

Always give the rabbit a way out. A hiding place it can reach, and never leave a dog on its own with rabbits (opens in a new tab).

Can I take just one?

Rabbits are a highly social species. The RSPCA's words (opens in a new tab): they need companionship as much as a healthy diet. Just one? Then you are its company.

Littermates already get along. They've been together since the day they were born, so a pair from one litter goes home used to each other.

To keep it that way, both need neutering. Rabbits hit puberty at about three to four months (opens in a new tab), and that's when siblings start to fight. The usual way through is to separate them then, neuter both, and reunite them a month after surgery; bonding again isn't guaranteed. We'll talk it through before you take a pair.

Two that didn't grow up together need a proper introduction. Both neutered first, then short meetings on neutral ground (opens in a new tab), a little longer each day. Breed doesn't change the steps. I'll walk you through it.

When can I take one home?

Eight weeks is what I aim for. Every listing shows them as eight squares, filled up to the rabbit's current week. When all eight are filled, the label reads "All eight weeks".

The test is the same every time. Off milk and eating hay and pellets on its own. Eating and drinking well, on the same food, for at least seven days. Holding or gaining weight when I weigh it, with nothing wrong with it that I can find. One that isn't there yet waits, whatever its age.

Eight weeks is when the changeover is done. Babies wean at four to six weeks and lose the protection of their mother's milk while their gut is still learning solid food. The House Rabbit Society says not to take them away “until they are a full eight weeks of age” (opens in a new tab). The RSPCA agrees (opens in a new tab): eight weeks, “fully weaned and independent”.

The risk is in those weeks, and it's rare. The Merck Veterinary Manual (opens in a new tab) puts enterotoxaemia, a severe gut disease, “primarily” in rabbits “4–8 weeks old”, and names diet and the stress of weaning. A new house and new food land in that same window. So the younger a rabbit goes, the more room there is for something to go wrong.

They go home eating what they eat here. Two weeks of that food goes in the bag. Nothing changes on day one.

A Ready date on a listing is the day I expect it to be ready. You'll get photos and video the whole way through, so the wait is easier.

How much grooming do they need?

Not much for a short coat. A brush once a week is plenty, a little more when they shed. Nails every few weeks.

A woolly coat needs more. Some of my rabbits have a longer, wavy coat, and a coat like that needs daily grooming (opens in a new tab) so it doesn't mat.

No baths. They keep themselves clean, and a bath is stressful and dangerous for a rabbit (opens in a new tab).

Indoors or outdoors?

Indoors, especially here. Rabbits handle cold far better than heat, and anything over the mid-80s for long is dangerous (opens in a new tab). A Tucson summer isn't survivable in a hutch.

An indoor pen with daily run-around time. That's what I recommend. Supervised, because rabbits chew cables.

Where do the rabbits live here?

The cages are for the mothers, not for everyone. The young rabbits are out running through the day, and so are the bucks (the males). A mother usually stays in, because she is nursing.

A nursing mother needs her nest box to stay put. A rabbit feeds her litter once or twice a day, and only when she feels safe (opens in a new tab). The box has to be where she left it, quiet, with nobody else in it. So a mother and her litter are housed on their own, as the university guide to raising rabbits (opens in a new tab) says.

Keeping them apart is how a pedigree stays true. Nothing breeds by accident here, so I know every rabbit's mother and father. That is what lets me tell you exactly what you are buying.

A cage is not a home for a pet rabbit. The welfare groups are right about that, and the indoors answer says the same: an indoor pen with room to run (opens in a new tab), and time out every day.

How long do they live?

8 to 12 years indoors, with good care. That is the House Rabbit Society's range (opens in a new tab). Spayed or neutered and kept indoors, about ten is typical.

Most pet rabbits don't get there. A study of 6,349 rabbits under vet care in England, a quarter of them lops, found the typical one died at 4.3 years (opens in a new tab). A Tokyo clinic's 898 cases put it at 7 years (opens in a new tab), with nearly one in five past nine.

The gap is care, not luck. Flystrike led the causes in England, with not eating next. Not eating is on my same-day emergency list, and you keep my number for life.

What if something goes wrong after pickup?

These signs are a same-day emergency: Not eating, no droppings, hunched and quiet, breathing hard, or loose watery droppings. Get to a rabbit-savvy vet. A rabbit that isn't eating can go from a little off to critical in hours (opens in a new tab). Don't wait to reach me first. Text me on the way and I'll help with whatever I can.

For anything that isn't urgent, call me. Whether you've had the rabbit three days or three years, I'd rather hear early than after it's serious.
