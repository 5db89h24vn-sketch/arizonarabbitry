About

Small on purpose, so every rabbit gets my time.

I'm William. I started with chinchillas, but they don't like being held. I wanted a pet that comes to you, and that led me to rabbits.

These litters are Holland Lop crosses and purebred Holland Lops. The crosses come from my Holland Lop and my Satin Angora. My first purebred Holland Lops were born 18 September 2026, each with its full pedigree.

Why the first weeks matter. In a study, babies handled briefly in their first week came up to people at weaning, and still did as adults (opens in a new tab). So every baby here is handled daily, and gets used to new people, sounds and places before it goes home.

That routine is what you're paying for. It makes a rabbit more likely to come to people.

Where the money goes. I buy breeding rabbits from winning lines at shows across the country, at thousands each, and fly home with them. Their babies go home here for a fraction of that, and every sale goes into the next line.

Every rabbit gets the same care, whatever its price. The aim is a rabbit good enough for a show table and just as happy on your couch.

More about the rabbitry

Some rabbits stay. Each line is bred to get better, and the ones that carry it forward stay with me. Every litter is on the litters page, back to 28 June 2026.

      When can I take one home?

      Eight weeks is what I aim for. Every listing shows the rabbit's age today, and whether it is open to reserve.

      The test is the same every time. Off milk and eating hay and pellets on its own. Eating and drinking well, on the same food, for at least seven days. Holding or gaining weight when I weigh it, with nothing wrong with it that I can find. One that isn't there yet waits, whatever its age.

      Eight weeks is when the changeover is done. Babies wean at four to six weeks and lose the protection of their mother's milk while their gut is still learning solid food. The House Rabbit Society says not to take them away “until they are a full eight weeks of age” (opens in a new tab). The RSPCA agrees (opens in a new tab): eight weeks, “fully weaned and independent”.

      The risk is in those weeks, and it's rare. The Merck Veterinary Manual (opens in a new tab) puts enterotoxaemia, a severe gut disease, “primarily” in rabbits “4–8 weeks old”, and names diet and the stress of weaning. A new house and new food land in that same window. So the younger a rabbit goes, the more room there is for something to go wrong.

      They go home eating what they eat here. Two weeks of that food goes in the bag. Nothing changes on day one.

      Where do the rabbits live here?

      The cages are for the mothers, not for everyone. The young rabbits are out running through the day, and so are the bucks (the males). A mother usually stays in, because she is nursing.

      A nursing mother needs her nest box to stay put. A rabbit feeds her litter once or twice a day, and only when she feels safe (opens in a new tab). The box has to be where she left it, quiet, with nobody else in it. So a mother and her litter are housed on their own, as the university guide to raising rabbits (opens in a new tab) says.

      Keeping them apart is how a pedigree stays true. Nothing breeds by accident here, so I know every rabbit's mother and father. That is what lets me tell you exactly what you are buying.

      A cage is not a home for a pet rabbit. The welfare groups are right about that, and the indoors answer says the same: an indoor pen with room to run (opens in a new tab), and time out every day.

      I have cats or dogs. Can a rabbit work?

      Often, yes, with slow introductions. Tell me what animals you have when you get in touch. If a rabbit isn't right for your home, I'll say so.

      They're used to a busy house. Here they hear household noise, spend time outside and meet new people. Your home is one more new place, not the first.

      Cats usually settle in. The House Rabbit Society says indoor cats typically will peacefully coexist (opens in a new tab) with rabbits after proper introductions. Start with the rabbit in a pen the cat can't reach into, and keep the cat's claws trimmed.

      With a dog, it depends on the dog. A calm dog that doesn't chase small animals can learn, on a lead and lying down, in short visits. Terriers and hunting breeds are the hardest. Supervision is required until 100% trust has been achieved (opens in a new tab).

      Always give the rabbit a way out. A hiding place it can reach, and never leave a dog on its own with rabbits (opens in a new tab).

      Rabbits are a highly social species. The RSPCA's words (opens in a new tab): they need companionship as much as a healthy diet. Just one? Then you are its company.

      Littermates already get along. They've been together since the day they were born, so a pair from one litter goes home used to each other.

      To keep it that way, both need neutering. Rabbits hit puberty at about three to four months (opens in a new tab), and that's when siblings start to fight. The usual way through is to separate them then, neuter both, and reunite them a month after surgery; bonding again isn't guaranteed. We'll talk it through before you take a pair.

      Two that didn't grow up together need a proper introduction. Both neutered first, then short meetings on neutral ground (opens in a new tab), a little longer each day. Breed doesn't change the steps. I'll walk you through it.

      Everyday careWhat to have ready, food, litter, grooming
      What should I have ready before it comes home?

      A pen indoors, in a room you use. A pen rather than a cage, with room to hop, and time out of it every day. Why indoors.

      A litter box with hay in it. Rabbits like to eat while they go, so hay in the box keeps them using it. How the box works.

      Water, hay and a box to hide in. Fresh water every day and grass hay always there. A cardboard box with a doorway cut in it helps a rabbit feel safe (opens in a new tab).

      Cables out of reach. Rabbits chew them. The House Rabbit Society's advice: cords in tubing or out of reach (opens in a new tab).

      The food comes with it. Two weeks of what it eats now, bamboo to chew and the care sheet. Everything that comes with it.

      Indoors or outdoors?

      Indoors, especially here. Rabbits handle cold far better than heat, and anything over the mid-80s for long is dangerous (opens in a new tab). A Tucson summer is too hot for a hutch outside.

      An indoor pen with daily run-around time. That's what I recommend. Supervised, because rabbits chew cables. What to have ready.

      What do they eat?

      Mostly hay, as much as it wants. Grass hay like timothy is about 85% of a grown rabbit's diet. The House Rabbit Society: unlimited fresh hay, every day (opens in a new tab).

      A grown rabbit also gets greens and a few pellets. At least three kinds of leafy greens a day, one new kind at a time, and a small amount of plain timothy pellets, not a mix with seeds or nuts.

      Fruit only as a treat. A small piece, once or twice a week.

      Fresh water, always. Changed every day.

      Its first two weeks, the same food as here. It goes home with two weeks of it. After that, anything new goes in slowly, one food at a time, because a sudden change upsets a rabbit's stomach. The care sheet goes through its diet in detail.

      Will it use a litter box?

      Usually, yes. Rabbits choose a corner to go in, so a box in that corner gets used. Put hay in the box or next to it (opens in a new tab), because they like to eat while they go.

      A young one takes a little patience. Older rabbits often learn faster, and spaying or neutering usually helps.

      A few droppings outside the box are normal. It's how a rabbit marks its space. Dry, hard droppings are nothing to worry about.

      Paper or wood-pellet litter, never clumping cat litter. A thin layer, with hay on top. Pine and cedar shavings are out too.

      How much grooming do they need?

      Not much for a short coat. A brush once a week is plenty, a little more when they shed. Nails every few weeks.

      A woolly coat needs more. Some of my rabbits have a longer, wavy coat, and a coat like that needs daily grooming (opens in a new tab) so it doesn't mat.

      No baths. They keep themselves clean, and a bath is stressful and dangerous for a rabbit (opens in a new tab).

      How long do they live?

      8 to 12 years indoors, with good care. That is the House Rabbit Society's range (opens in a new tab). Spayed or neutered and kept indoors, about ten is typical.

      Care makes the difference. A study of 6,349 pet rabbits in England found the typical one died at 4.3 years (opens in a new tab). A Tokyo clinic's 898 cases put it at 7 years (opens in a new tab). Flystrike led the causes in England, with not eating next. Not eating is on my same-day emergency list, and you keep my number for life.

      What if something goes wrong after pickup?

A rabbit that isn't eating can go from a little off to critical in hours (opens in a new tab). Don't wait to reach me first. Text me on the way and I'll help with whatever I can.

      For anything that isn't urgent, call me. Whether you've had the rabbit three days or three years, I'd rather hear early than after it's serious.
