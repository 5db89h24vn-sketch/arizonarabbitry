About

Comfort with people is built in the first weeks, long before you meet them.

I'm William, and I came to rabbits through chinchillas. A chinchilla doesn't want to be held. Keeping them taught me what I was after: an animal that comes to you.

These litters are Holland Lop crosses. Purebreds go on sale late October or early November, once I've seen which ones I'm keeping as they grow. A farmer sold me a Satin Angora, and a Holland Lop followed. The purebred lines come next: Netherland Dwarfs are here, Holland Lops next, then another Angora, kept separate.

Some rabbits never go up for sale. Each line is bred to get better, and the ones that carry it forward stay. Every litter is on the litters page from its first day, back to the first one, 28 June 2026.

Rabbits are prey animals, and hands have to be learned. Kits handled briefly at feeding time in their first week come up to people at weaning, and still do as adults. Kits nobody handled avoid the hand and grow up afraid of people.

Those weeks are long before you could meet them, so they're mine. I keep it going every day after, with other people. A rabbit calm only with its breeder isn't socialized.

Handling doesn't make a rabbit love being lifted. Most never do. What it does is make a rabbit that comes to people instead of running from them. Each one is still its own animal. The routine is what you're paying for.

The routine here

What happens before you meet them

The whole routine, week by week. Every listing shows which week its rabbit is in. Ask me about any part of it.

Week 1–2

First handling

Short, gentle sessions within days of birth, nest kept warm and mostly undisturbed.

Week 3–5

Carrying becomes normal

Lifting with a hand under the back and bottom every time, and long stretches at floor level, which is where a rabbit is most at ease. Leaving the ground is the fear most pet rabbits never lose.

Week 6–8

New people, new noise

Visitors, children, the noise of a house, and the carrier with the door open so it stops being a trap. Ordinary life at their own pace, with somewhere to retreat to whenever they want it.

Going home

When they're ready

There's no set age for it. A kit goes home once it's off milk and eating hay and pellets on its own, and has been eating and drinking well for at least a week. I weigh it, and it has to be holding or gaining, with nothing wrong with it. Some get there at eight weeks and some sooner. One that isn't there yet waits.

Send me a text or a note. Tell me a little about your home and who the rabbit is for. I'll tell you which one fits, and I'll say so if the answer is to wait for the next litter.

Then come and meet them. The visit is free and there's no booking fee. Nothing has to be decided on the day.

Why do they cost what they cost?

The price on the photo is the price you pay. No appointment fee, no no-show fee, no charge to visit, nothing added at pickup.

It covers more than the rabbit. Daily handling from the day it's born, and a health check you watch me do. Two weeks of the food it's already eating, a written care sheet, and my number for as long as you have it.

Colour and temperament set the price. Rarer colours cost more. Right now Butterscotch, a fawn doe, is $375.

You can find a cheaper rabbit. Classifieds are full of them under a hundred. What you pay for here is a rabbit that's already used to being picked up, and someone who still answers the phone at month seven.

Can I take two?

Yes. Two together: the second is 20% off. Same litter or not, the pair price is the same.

It also holds if you reserve a littermate before the first one goes home. A second rabbit bought later on is full price.

Littermates already get along. They've been together since the day they were born, so a pair from one litter goes home used to each other.

To keep it that way, both need neutering. Rabbits hit puberty at about three to four months, and that's when siblings start to fight. Neuter both before then and they can stay together. We'll talk it through before you take a pair.

Two that didn't grow up together need a proper introduction. Both neutered first, then short meetings on neutral ground, a little longer each day. Breed doesn't change the steps. I'll walk you through it.

Is there a deposit, and what happens to it?

$50 holds a rabbit and comes off the price. Once it's paid, that rabbit is yours until pickup.

Reservations open at four weeks old. Before that, a kit's sex and colour are still a guess, and I won't take money for a guess.

Change your mind: the $50 moves with you. It isn't refunded, because holding a rabbit means turning other people away. It moves to another rabbit or to the next litter.

First switch free. After that, $25 each. That's half the deposit. Changing your mind once is normal. Every switch after that puts a rabbit back on the list weeks late, and the fee is what that costs me.

Three things get the $50 back, no argument:

  • The rabbit gets ill or doesn't make it while still in my care.
  • I'm the one who cancels, for any reason.
  • The rabbit isn't what I told you it was. Wrong sex, a colour clearly different from the listing, not the rabbit you reserved, or it fails the health check at pickup.

Normal growing up isn't a refund. Colour and markings can still shift after four weeks. I'll say what's certain when you reserve, and you'll get photos the whole way.

Change your mind at pickup? Whole $50 back. That holds for the first rabbit you reserve. After a switch, we settle on what it cost me, fair to both of us. I'd rather you said so than took home a rabbit you weren't sure about.

When can I take one home?

When it's ready. There's no set age. I decide kit by kit. Some are ready at eight weeks and some sooner.

The test is the same every time. Off milk and eating hay and pellets on its own. Eating and drinking well for at least seven days. Holding or gaining weight when I weigh it, with nothing wrong with it.

The reason is the changeover. Coming off milk is the stretch where their gut is still learning the job. A new house and new food on top of that is a lot to ask. So a kit that isn't there yet waits here, where nothing else is changing.

They go home eating what they eat here. Two weeks of that food goes in the bag. Nothing changes on day one.

A Ready date is the date I expect. You'll get photos and video the whole way through, so the wait is easier.

I've never had a rabbit. Is that a problem?

No. First-timers are often who I enjoy working with most, because they actually read the care sheet.

Rabbits aren't a low-maintenance starter pet. They need room to run and time with you every day, plus a vet who treats rabbits. If that fits your house, they're one of the best animals you can live with.

Rabbits and kids do well together with a grown-up in charge. Kids sit on the floor and let the rabbit come to them. The lifting is an adult's job, and the daily care is too.

How much grooming do they need?

Not much for a short coat. A brush once a week is plenty, a little more when they moult. Nails every few weeks.

No baths. They keep themselves clean, and a bath is stressful and dangerous for a rabbit.

Indoors or outdoors?

Indoors, especially here. Rabbits handle cold far better than heat, and anything over the mid-80s for long is dangerous. A Tucson summer isn't survivable in a hutch.

An indoor pen with daily run-around time. That's what I recommend. Supervised, because rabbits chew cables.

How long do they live?

8 to 12 years indoors, with good care. That is the House Rabbit Society's range. Spayed or neutered and kept indoors, about ten is typical.

Most pet rabbits don't get there. A study of 6,349 rabbits under vet care in England, a quarter of them lops, found the typical one died at 4.3 years. A Tokyo clinic's 898 cases put it at 7 years, with nearly one in five past nine.

The gap is care, not luck. Flystrike led the causes in England, with not eating next. Not eating is on my same-day emergency list, and you keep my number for life.

Can you look after my rabbit while I'm away?

Yes, for rabbits I've bred. One rabbit is $20 a day, dropping to $15 a day after the first week. A bonded pair sharing a pen is $30 a day, dropping to $22.

Priced by the day on purpose. You only pay for the days you're away, and you can extend without renegotiating anything.

Food, bedding and handling are included. Hay, pellets, clean bedding and the same daily handling they grew up with, and photos while you're gone.

Why only my own rabbits: RHDV2 is in Arizona's wild rabbits. It survives for months in the environment and travels on shoes and clothing, and it's often fatal. One outside rabbit could bring it to every rabbit here, including the babies waiting to go to their homes.

A closed property is also why I can promise a healthy rabbit at pickup.

Rabbit from somewhere else? Ask me anyway and I'll point you to a Tucson boarder or exotic vet who takes outside rabbits.

I'm not in Tucson. Can you meet partway or come to me?

Get in touch and we'll work it out. Tell me roughly where you are and I'll tell you what's easiest on the rabbit. Sometimes that's meeting in the middle, sometimes I drive to you.

Heat is the one thing I'm firm about. What matters is the temperature the rabbit would be sitting in on the way, and that has to stay under the mid-80s. If a trip isn't safe on the day, we move it, and I'll say so.

Prices change, so their list is the one that counts. They ask every client to spay or neuter, which I would tell you to do anyway.

The exotic hospital is the name people think of first. Arizona Exotic Animal Hospital on Grant Road sees rabbits too. For the same care, Santa Cruz is the much better option for its price. If Santa Cruz can't fit you in, the exotic hospital asks you to call for anything within two days: (520) 305-0055.

A sick rabbit does not wait for an appointment. If you see any of the same-day signs in the next answer, call whoever can see you today.

What if something goes wrong after pickup?

These signs are a same-day emergency: Not eating, no droppings, hunched and quiet, breathing hard, or loose watery droppings. Get to a rabbit-savvy vet. A rabbit that isn't eating can go from off-colour to critical in hours. Don't wait to reach me first. Text me on the way and I'll help with whatever I can.

For anything that isn't urgent, call me. Whether you've had the rabbit three days or three years, I'd rather hear early than after it's serious.

My health check before every rabbit leaves. We do it together at pickup with the rabbit in your hands: ears, teeth, eyes, weight and coat. If it doesn't pass, it doesn't go home, and you get your deposit back in full.

Once a rabbit goes home, the sale is final. I'd rather you read that here than find it out later. Nothing about the rabbit is hidden from you on the day, and you leave with everything on the list above.

If it simply isn't working out, call me. That isn't a refund, but it isn't the end of it either. I'd rather help you find it the right home than have it stay somewhere it isn't wanted.

I'd rather promise what I can deliver than print a guarantee with fine print. A rabbit that was healthy when you took it, and me reachable for the whole of its life.

The deposit refunds are separate. All of those happen before a rabbit ever leaves here. They're in the deposit answer.
